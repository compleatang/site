After reading so much AI drivel my eyes were about to fall out, I have decided it was time to write again. This blog will be a chance for me to write again which is a skill and habit that has been sorely lacking from my habits these last years. And it's something that I now quite miss doing.

I think that we as humans are going to increasingly understand writing that has been crafted by humans and that which has been crafted by machines. I already have a few turn of phrases that just make me want to throw all the GPUs into the pits of volcanic hell because they're more overused than a summer pop song from my youth. This is my space to avoid that and to produce something authentically human into the world. Although I do not claim that the styling and site management itself will not be deeply robot managed. But the content and writing will be all me (a human).

---

For the last dozen years I've been trying to startup. The next entries in this blog will be about many of the things that I've learned along that journey. Mostly about all the ways that I fucked up and failed to actually, well, startup. It's about failure. And my increasingly toxic relationship with that word. 

In the holidays of 2013 I went home to the US and was spending time with my family. As I had been in a bit of transition phase at the time, my father asked me what I really wanted to do "with my life" -- as father's are wont to do. I told him that I liked being a lawyer but what I really wanted to be doing was to build software. I think I said "run a software company" rather than "build software" two notions I learned over the years are quite different. And that was the start of an unlikely journey into this world of entrepreneurship.

Since that time I've had an astounding record of misses. Or near misses. Or learnings. Or, just failures. I've been in crypto since 2013 but never successfully aura farmed my way to generational wealth. Initially its cause I was "interested in the tech" (which I was). I focused on developer tools before that existed and folks made millions on launchpads and other tooling. I helped build the first indexers before folks made billions on indexing and such hosted services. I built non-public blockchains before any of the enterprises came knocking. I built systems of smart contracts before anyone knew you could even connect these things together. I built NFT marketplaces, crypto-legal solutions, and trading systems. Nothing worked.

I was right about the market development on almost every cycle. And yet I continued to fail to succeed. I failed my investors, my family, and myself. Why? I don't believe there is a single reason other than: markets create competition and competition rewards hard work alongside right place at the right time alongside a bit of luck. 

---

What I do believe is that trying with all you can find and failing to achieve leaves you with a fundamental choice: continue or change. That's of course a trope. You and learn from your failure and all that is of course true, but that trope buries the deeper, harder question. In my experience the hardest part of that choice is to understand when you've failed.

I'm not sure I've been able to develop a feel for when failure is actually achieved. On the one hand I've watched folks work on projects that clearly were going to take a long time and slog it out in the desert even when nobody believed them -- at times for years -- and come out the otherside successfully. Digital Assets is a great example of this. Securitize another. Both of those companies I competed with at one time or another; eventually, giving up or changing course for one reason or another. Both became very successful companies and unlike me did not change course fundamentally. That road is very very hard. It takes capital and patient investors. Both of which I did have, but I think that it takes something else that I fundamentally lacked for much of my entrepreneurship years: an ironclad belief in the efficacy of your work. 

In company building or in product building it's very very easy to fall in love with the honeymoon period that happens when it's all new. A new codebase allows you to reset your possibilities. A new deck template lets you shed the old ideas that you feel have hampered your development. A new set of hires brings a great energy. When you feel the pain of the build, of the slog, of lingering sales, of pending failure, it's super easy to sink into the idea of the new. It almost becomes a tractor beam pulling your mental energy into that new ship. Overcoming that tractor beam appeal of the new requires belief that you weren't wrong, you just weren't right in that moment. It requires you to have an understanding of what the market is going to do next, how you can capture that, and a plan to move to the next identified steps along the way.

I had many shortcomings as a product builder and as a company builder. But perhaps the biggest of these was that I never sunk my teeth into something so hard that changing course would have required the massive pain of teeth removal and eventual dental surgery. I think I never really, fundamentally could land in a place where I believed in my bones that what I was doing would actually work. And so many of the things I was doing at various junctures did work. Rarely at the time I was doing them, but they did work. I was right. But so often by the time I was write, I had moved on. 

As I've tried to heal myself from extreme burnout and immense feelings of inadequacy I've been spending time reflecting on why I could never get to a place of simple conviction in what I was doing. Clearly the market didn't give validation. Investors gave a bit of validation. Employees gave some validation. But really, as a founder, you are the wellspring of conviction -- if it's not within you then it's exceedingly rare for anybody else to be able to give it to you. 

My history as a generalist I think is one of my hindrances. I've seen so many succeed where I failed for basically one of two reasons: (a) they had early market or investor validation where I failed to find that -- usually because their timing was better than mine, or (b) they were not generalists historically like I was but rather specialists that believed in what they were doing deeply in their core because they had felt the pain and knew the cost of the problem they were solving.

These two things I've come to undrstand are the core tenants of successful entrepreneurship. You must, fundamentally, have one or the other if you're going to succeed in this extreme business sport.
